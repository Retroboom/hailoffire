A tabletop game of corporate heists, street survival, and the tech that's wired straight into your nervous system.

FRONT MATTER

Welcome to The City

Prepare yourself to enter the dystopian urban sprawl of The City, ruled by corporate elites, paid-off officials, and private security firms. There's the Haves and the Have-Nots, and those who refuse to play by the rules. Street gangs and crime bosses play their own game. Technology is everywhere, inescapable, and often installed directly into the body. The culture is high-tech and hyper-aesthetic. Whether it's the newest designer fashion or an LED face tattoo, you'd better make a statement.

The City can give you anything you've ever wanted, for the right price. It can make you rich, powerful, invincible… or it can chew you up and spit you out.

In Hot Wired, players take on the role of Cyberpunks: a diverse group of individuals fighting to survive and thrive in a hostile world, by any means necessary. The Mega Corps think they own everything, including you, but with advanced technology at your fingertips, you and your partners-in-crime will use your skills to break into their facilities, hack into their systems, steal their secrets, and disrupt their operations. And maybe make good money doing it. But you'll also have to deal with the dangerous world of the streets, where rival gangs, ruthless Bosses, and rogue AI all pose a threat.

Welcome to the gritty, high-tech world of cyberpunk, where the only thing more dangerous than the corporations is the technology they wield.

Are you ready to make a name for yourself?

Player principles

  • Portray a compelling character
  • Engage with the world
  • Play to find out what happens
  • Be bold, take risks
  • Embrace difficulty, setback, and failure
  • Build on what others have said
  • Give others a chance to shine

When in doubt…

  • Visualize the situation
  • Ask the GM for clarification
  • Think about what you want, what is your goal
  • Consider your strengths and weaknesses
  • Look to others for ideas
  • Go with the obvious choice, the interesting choice, or the meaningful choice

Game master principles

  • Make the world high-tech, low life
  • Fill the characters' lives with action, intrigue, and complication
  • Address the characters, not the players
  • Ask questions, and use the answers
  • Be a fan of the characters

When a PC suffers, or the table waits…

  • Introduce a new threat
  • Hit them with an established threat
  • Make their lives more complicated
  • Give them a difficult choice
  • Use up their resources
  • Always follow up with "What do you do?"
PART 01 // RESOLUTION

Core Mechanisms

Two pools of dice. Two colors. Everything else in this book is what you're rolling for.

The Task Roll

Many things Players will declare that their character does are simple and stress-free, with essentially no danger of failure. If a Player Character PC is in no immediate danger and it's announced that they tie their shoe, there's no reason to assume it isn't done. Other times, when risk is involved and the outcome is not guaranteed, Players will make a Task Roll to help determine what happens.

Every Task Roll consists of rolling two dice pools: the Boost Pool, rolled by the Player, and the Challenge Pool, rolled by the GM.

Boost pool — rolled by player

Roll 1d6 for each Boost, such as:

  • A single relevant Background
  • Each valid part of a Skill Description
  • Each relevant tag on Cyberware and/or one or more pieces of Primo Kit
  • Each situational tag that can be exploited (#highground, #hidden, etc.)

Challenge pool — rolled by GM

Roll 1d6 for:

  • Current Riskometer bonus (Heat ÷ 5, rounded down — see Part 08)
  • Each Condition and unstabilized Injury
  • Each relevant stabilized Injury
  • Each situational tag that would increase difficulty (#outnumbered, #distracted, etc.)

Both pools are rolled and compared. Each die showing a 1 or a 6 counts as a Success.

Resolution
  • Hit — more Successes in the Boost pool than the Challenge pool: score Hits equal to the difference. (Extra Hits after the first are Bonus Hits.)
  • Miss — more Successes in the Challenge pool: score no Hits and suffer a Consequence.
  • Push — equal Successes in both pools (including 0): score 1 Hit and suffer a Consequence.
Field note

Anything can go sideways in The City, sometimes in your favor. A rolled 1 isn't clean skill, it's a glitch: a misfire that still grazes, a guard's reflexes failing half a second too late. Narrate 1-successes as something breaking weird, not as a sharpshooter's instinct.

For a Task to be successfully achieved, a certain number of Hits (often written as 2✱) must be generated. Some Tasks resolve quickly, like leaping out of the way of a vehicle, and require only 1✱, while others might take more time, like breaking into a safe or repairing a damaged drone, and require 2✱ or more.

The required number of ✱ does not need to be generated in a single roll, and ✱ can accumulate over several Task Rolls. However, the more rolls Players make to achieve a stated outcome, the more they risk one or several of those rolls generating Consequences, making their situation more difficult. GMs declare the number of ✱ a Task requires before a player commits to it. If a PC rolls more ✱ than the Task requires in a single roll, the PC may request some additional effect of their choosing, appropriate to the Task.

Clocks

For any Task that requires more than 1✱ and is likely to take more than one roll to resolve, track progress with a Clock: a circle divided into segments equal to the Hits required. Fill in one segment per Hit scored. A Clock keeps accumulating progress visible to the whole table, and works equally well for breaking into a safe, hacking a hardened network, talking down a hostile boss, or grinding through a tough enemy in a fight.

PC aid

PCs can (and should!) aid each other when attempting Tasks, especially when a Task is either particularly dangerous or complicated. If the aiding PC can explain how they're actually providing assistance, and the GM agrees the action is possible (a PC can't provide covering fire if they aren't armed), they can help in one of two ways: a PC with a relevant Background, Cyberware, or Primo Kit may offer 1 Boost die outright. A PC without one of those three may still try to help, but must make a Task Roll of their own against 1 Challenge die and risk a Consequence; if they score a Hit, they may offer that Boost die instead.

When a Player declares their PC is aiding another's Task, their character genuinely attempts whatever action they described, successful or not, and it should be reflected in the fictional positioning of that PC. Regardless of how many eligible Boosts an aiding PC might have, they only ever add 1 total Boost. Whenever possible, the PC best suited to a Task should perform it directly, to maximize the dice being rolled.

Consequences

Each time a PC makes a Task Roll, they risk the GM introducing a Consequence. A Consequence impedes, provokes, or harms the PCs somehow in their current context. Consequences can manifest in any way that makes sense given the situation, and while they often punish characters for mistakes, they more importantly add pressure to the party and their circumstances, building suspense, excitement (or dread), and surprise, all of which push the story forward.

Examples
  • Introduce a new threat
  • Use up a resource
  • Inflict a Condition or Injury
  • Increase the Riskometer

Grit

PCs start with a pool of 3 Grit. Grit may be spent to increase the result of a Boost die by 1. Multiple Grit may be spent on one roll to increase the result of more than one die, and/or to increase a single die by more than 1. Spent Grit is restored by invoking a character's Bugs.

Because Grit is spent at the player's discretion, it only ever helps: it adds to a die's result, it never subtracts, so it can turn a non-Success into a 6 but never turn a 1 into something worse. In practice, players spend Grit on dice sitting at 5 (cheapest, 1 Grit) or stack it on a 2, 3, or 4 when the cost is worth it. There's never a reason to spend Grit on a die already showing a 1.

PART 02 // WHO YOU ARE

Creating a Character

Made of words and description first, numbers second.

Characters in Hot Wired are made up less of numbers and stats, and more of words and descriptions. Essentially, all a player needs to do is describe their character in a few sentences and they're most of the way there.

Backgrounds

Your character's occupation, where they grew up, training or education, organizations, and so on. These inform what kinds of actions they have experience and proficiency performing. When creating a character, choose 3 Backgrounds.

Skill descriptions

After declaring your character's 3 Backgrounds, describe one or more specific Skills originating from each. Loosely, in sentence form (don't worry too much about the grammar), define an action your character is good at taking because of their background.

Example

Background — Street Gang Muscle
Skill Description — I use guns to shoot people at short range.

We then break that sentence into specific parts, each narrowing the focus further:

1 · I use guns   |   2 · to shoot people   |   3 · at short range

In play, each part of the sentence adds a Boost to a relevant Task Roll. You must move through the sentence sequentially: part 1 must be relevant to the Task Roll first, then part 2, then part 3.

Example 1

I'm shooting at someone trying to escape into a car a few meters away, and the GM says it requires a Task Roll.

+1 · Street Gang Muscle background +1 · "I use guns" +1 · "to shoot people" +1 · "at short range"

Total: 4 dice (assuming no other Boosts)

Example 2

I'm shooting at someone trying to escape into a car one city block away. "At short range" no longer applies.

+1 · Street Gang Muscle background +1 · "I use guns" +1 · "to shoot people"

Total: 3 dice (assuming no other Boosts)

Example 3

I'm throwing a wine bottle at an enemy running past me. The Background applies (close enough to muscle work), but part 1, "I use guns," doesn't. Since it fails, no later part can apply either.

+1 · Street Gang Muscle background

Total: 1 die (assuming no other Boosts)

Remember: each part of a Skill Description must narrow the part before it. If a later part is always applicable no matter the circumstance, it's not narrowing enough.

Doesn't count

1 · I use handguns  |  2 · or really any gun  |  3 · to shoot people

"Or really any gun" widens the focus instead of narrowing it, and provides no Boost.

Playable narrowing

Every part of a Skill Description is really a plain claim: my character gets a bonus under these specific conditions, ones another character wouldn't get. Test each later part with one question: if the part before it succeeds, could the Task still succeed without this part also being true? If the honest answer is "no, winning always looks like this," it isn't narrowing, it's the Task's own success restated in nicer words, and it'll fire on every single attempt of that Task, background or not. "Into telling me the truth" passes this test: a successful intimidation can also end in silence, compliance, or a handed-over item, so demanding the truth specifically excludes those other, equally successful outcomes. "Until they're stable" fails it: there's no other kind of successful outcome to working on a dying patient, so it's true of every Stabilize attempt anyone ever makes.

When there's no genuine alternate outcome to exclude, reach for a circumstance instead, a precondition that's only sometimes true when a character could attempt the Task at all: mid-firefight rather than after, with scrap on hand rather than a full kit, once a target's already #Pinned or a Mag's already empty. Either kind of part is fine. Restating the goal as if it were a condition is not.

Gate on the state, not the cause

When a part leans on something a previous roll produced, name the result, not who caused it or how. #Pinned doesn't care whose shot landed it, and a part built the same way survives no matter which PC, or which piece of Kit, actually handled the danger. A part that only works "once Ohm's hacked their comms specifically" is fragile: it goes dead the instant a different PC solves the same problem a different way, or Ohm just isn't in the scene.

The state also has to leave the danger standing. A part that only applies "once the exit's already clear" can never actually pay off, because a clear exit means there's no risk left, which means no Task Roll gets made there at all. Watch that exact mistake get fixed, draft by draft, for a getaway driver's chase Skill:

  1. "once the crew's already cleared our exit" — fails. No risk left means no roll ever happens here to attach the Boost to.
  2. "once Ohm's already scrambled their comms" — fails. Dead the moment a different PC solves it a different way, or Ohm's not around.
  3. "once we've already knocked one of them off our tail" — works, but narrows the cause more than it needs to.
  4. "once the crew's already bought me a few seconds" — works. A small, real, nameable edge. The danger's still live, and it doesn't care who bought the time or how.
Would the GM ever call for this roll?

Before locking a part in, picture the actual moment at the table. A part only pays off if there's a real scene where the GM says "alright, roll for that." If the honest answer is "the GM would probably just let that happen for free," the part isn't attached to anything, per the very first rule in this book: no risk, no roll, nothing to Boost. "I read a room's exits and blind spots" is calm scene-setting most of the time, nobody rolls dice to notice a door exists, so it needs real stakes bolted on ("before the shooting starts") before it ever actually fires. If you can't picture the table moment where this specific roll gets made, the part is decoration, not mechanics.

When creating a character, you have 8 total points to spend on Skill Description parts, one point per part. Split them among many Skill Descriptions or commit them all to a couple. Skill Descriptions may also branch, giving your character extra versatility within certain situations.

Bugs

These are the compulsions PCs most struggle with. Players may invoke a character's Bugs in the narrative to restore lost Grit. Bugs take one of two forms: the character is compelled to a certain action, or the character refuses to perform a certain action. Characters should have at least two Bugs.

Roll 1d6 first: 1–3 means Compelled to…, 4–6 means Refuses to…. Then roll 1d20 below for the specific theme, and combine it with whichever stem you rolled, the same 20 entries work equally well either way (Compelled to follow orders reads just as easily as Refuses to follow orders).

Invoking Bugs

To regain lost Grit, a Player may invoke a character's Bug by describing how it negatively affects an impending Task. When this happens: the next roll functions as normal, except that a Consequence is guaranteed, and the PC's Grit pool is fully restored.

PART 03 // FLESH AND CHROME

Body & Hardware

What breaks in you, and what you replace it with.

Conditions and injuries

GMs may inflict Conditions and Injuries on PCs as a Consequence.

Conditions are emotional statuses that impose +1 Challenge Die to all Task Rolls the PC makes. The GM removes the status once the PC takes some action that plays out the condition, usually to negative effect (running away for "Frightened," for instance). Example Conditions: Shaken, Frightened, Exhausted, Confused, Stressed, Frustrated.

Injuries refer to serious physical harm, and stay with the character until treated during Downtime. Until stabilized, each Injury adds +1 Challenge Die to all Task Rolls the PC makes.

A single Consequence can inflict multiple Injuries if the GM believes it makes sense: a fall from a one-story roof might cause #SprainedAnkle, while a fall from three stories might cause #BrokenLeg, #ImpailedShoulder, and #Concussion all at once.

PCs can attempt to Stabilize an Injury as a Task. An appropriate consumable item (bandages, injections, inhalers — it's sci-fi) adds +1 Boost. A PC stabilizing themselves still takes the +1 Challenge from the injury itself; a PC stabilizing someone else does not. Though not healed, a stabilized Injury only affects Task Rolls relevant to that Injury.

Down

A PC carrying 3 unstabilized Injuries is Down: unconscious, pinned, or otherwise removed from active participation in the scene until stabilized. A Down PC can be stabilized by another PC's aid or by Medical Treatment during Downtime (see The Grind).

If a Down PC suffers a 4th unstabilized Injury before being stabilized, that's the kind of extreme circumstance PC Death is built for.

A GM looking to apply pressure against a Down PC, without calling for PC Death, can advance the Riskometer instead: the danger of the moment stays real without requiring more rolls against a character who's already out of the fight.

PC death

It's assumed that PCs are unlikely to avoid getting banged up, shot, burned, and broken, but it's also assumed that PC death is reserved for extreme circumstances: either there's simply no way to narratively justify survival, a Player decides their character will make the ultimate sacrifice, or some other similarly epic scenario. Adjust to the desires of your table.

Cyberware

Installing cybernetics is as common as upgrading your cell phone (albeit a bit more intrusive) and is often just as superficial.

PCs start with three Cyberware Boosts on any number of installations: cybernetic eyes that see in the dark, robotic legs that kick through concrete or leap fences, or just about anything else you can think of.

As always, Boosts require GM approval. Especially powerful or strange Cyberware might be granted, but with additional negative tags attached. During Downtime, PCs may attempt to install, upgrade, replace, or repair Cyberware for a price. Boosts on Cyberware should be described as actions, starting with a verb.

Cyber eyes

  • Night vision — see clearly in the dark
  • Magnification — see clearly at distance or at small scale
  • Video recorder — record what you see
  • Thermal — see through smoke or behind a wall
  • Aim assist — overlay lead time and bullet drop
  • Bounty intel — overlay public criminal records on whoever you're looking at

Cyber arms

  • Synthetic grafts — superhuman upper-body strength
  • Implanted blades — retractable forearm blades
  • Implanted firearm — retractable forearm firearm
  • Implanted tools — retractable forearm toolkit
  • Implanted shield — retractable forearm shield
  • Grappling hand launcher — launch your hand via cable up to 100 feet; can pull you and one other up to the anchor point (no guarantee it holds)

Cyber ears

  • Signal isolation — isolate the source of a radio signal
  • Amplification — boost sounds within a negotiated radius
  • Audio recorder — record what you hear
  • Voice analysis — identify traits in a voice (identity, hostility, fear)
  • Perfect balance — maintain your center of gravity almost anywhere
  • Detection — cancel ambient noise to detect audible changes

Neural implants

  • Neural interface — breach networks by connecting your brain via cable; navigate data and devices on the network
  • Remote drone control — control a paired drone (pairing with one you've practiced with is wise)
  • Implanted comms — receive and transmit verbal or text comms without making a sound
  • Data storage — store files where you won't lose them: inside your head
  • Reaction time reducer — react to stimuli at superhuman speed
  • Chem balancer — resist chemicals and drugs affecting perception or mood

Cyber legs

  • Speed boost — run fast
  • Jump boost — jump high and far
  • Gripping feet — climb sheer walls, hold a beer with your feet
  • Concealed compartment — conceal objects up to a set size; retrieval takes time depending on access

Other

  • Subdermal plating — ignore one piercing/cutting/burning Injury per day
  • Skeletal reinforcement — ignore one bone-breaking Injury per day
  • EMP blaster — disable electronics in a radius (1 use, recharges during Downtime)
  • Thermal controls — adjust ambient temperature within 5ft, ±50°F, immune to negative effects
  • Oxygen compressors — hold your breath ~10 minutes, fatigue-free
  • Magnetized hands — attract metal objects in a set radius; needs GM-negotiated control
PART 04 // LOADOUT

Basic & Primo Kit

What you carry, and how much of it fits.

PCs have access to any Basic Kit that wouldn't be hard to come by for someone with their Backgrounds. Basic Kit offers permission to attempt certain Tasks. A shotgun lets a PC shoot that, a car lets them drive there, a phone lets them call them. Basic Kit does not inherently offer a Boost.

Primo Kit is any tool with a Boost assigned to it. If relevant to a Task Roll, Players add a d6 to their Boost pool. Primo Kit with a tag irrelevant to the current Task can still be used as Basic Kit (the Task can happen, just without the Boost).

Example

A car is Basic Kit. A car with the Boost #suped is Primo Kit. Escaping a violent pursuer, the car lets you drive away under fire (1d6 total). With #suped, you attempt the same escape with +1d6 (2d6 total).

Encumbrance

PCs have a number of Equipment Slots for what they're carrying. In average circumstances, PCs have 3 Equipment Slots, accounting for what fits in their hands, pockets, or slung over a shoulder. Certain Backgrounds and Cyberware might offer more; some situations result in fewer. Items concealable in a closed fist take up 0 slots; items over 5 pounds or requiring two hands take up 2 slots (or more). Slots can be filled with Packs (backpacks, tac-vests, duffle bags) that offer additional slots while carried, though carrying them will likely draw attention or hinder quick, agile Tasks.

CarriedSlots
Standard clothing3 equipment slots
Tac-vest+4 equipment slots
Backpack+6 equipment slots
Duffle bag+10 equipment slots

If a PC's slots are full and they decide to carry more, they receive an additional Challenge Die on all rolls. The GM can also simply declare the PC isn't able to carry any more than they currently have.

Quantum kit

Other than Packs and Primo Kit, Players aren't required to declare what other equipment they're carrying, and can be assumed to have taken what their character thinks they'll need: Quantum Kit. Players can declare at any time that they have a piece of Basic Kit on them, listing it and filling the appropriate Equipment Slots as if it had always been there. If that Kit is used up, dropped, or lost, those slots can't hold Quantum Kit again until the PC resupplies somewhere appropriate, like a safehouse or a stocked vehicle.

PART 05 // ON THE STREET

Combat & Social 'Combat'

Guns, blades, morale, and the art of being convincing.

Flow of combat

As soon as bullets fly or punches are thrown (by PCs or otherwise), the GM should pause the scene, describe each NPC's current intent (at least to the degree the PCs might perceive), and ask each player what they're doing or about to do. The GM then resolves all the action in a loosely simultaneous manner. Combative Tasks resolve like all other Tasks, with Boost and Challenge dice added as makes sense, and Consequences assigned as they logically follow. PCs should use the pause before action to quickly strategize and leverage their strengths to give each other advantages.

Combat is dangerous for everyone involved, and Players should know that putting their characters in harm's way can regularly result in the Consequence of Injury.

Combat consequences

Consequences should scale to how much a PC chooses to expose themselves to danger. If they're receiving fire, a Consequence for diving for cover should be less severe than a Consequence for choosing to shoot back instead.

Weapons & ranges

Weapons are categorized as Guns, Melee, or Explosives. Every weapon has a designated Range at which it's most effective; using it outside that range is at GM discretion, resulting in additional Challenge dice if possible at all.

Rapid fire — when shooting, a Player can declare their character is rapid firing, adding +2 Boost dice if semi-auto or +4 Boost dice if full auto. After rapid firing, the weapon's mag is empty and needs reloading.

Pinned — unless the target is actively not trying to evade, the first ✱ of a shooting Task Roll doesn't harm the target, it causes them to become #Pinned instead. Pinned characters rush behind the nearest cover and suffer a 1-die penalty (+1 Challenge to Pinned PCs, +1 Boost if Pinned NPCs are acted against) on everything they do until they succeed at an action or the situation changes. If 2 or more ✱ are rolled on a single roll, each ✱ after the first harms the target (or achieves whatever effect the shooter was going for).

Mags — guns need a Mag to fire. An empty Mag (from Rapid Fire or a Consequence) prevents shooting until reloaded, which must be declared and might require a roll under pressure. One or three extra Mags can usually be concealed on the body; more than that is visibly strapped. More than 10 Mags means a Backpack or Duffle Bag.

Ammunition types — all guns can fire explosive, armor-piercing, poison, stun, EMP, and similar rounds. Mags loaded with special ammunition are Primo Kit. Successfully buying or making Primo Ammunition nets 3 Mags worth.

Heavy weapons & heavy armor — concrete walls, armored vehicles, and similar are assumed to carry the #heavyarmor Boost, and are only vulnerable to a weapon with the #heavyweapon Boost.

Melee weapons

Anything hard or sharp enough to bludgeon or cut someone to death is a potential Melee weapon. Characters don't worry about running out of Mags, making Melee a strong option when stealth matters. Usually limited to Close range, though many are still deadly thrown at Near range — remembering that throwing a weapon disarms its wielder (and potentially arms the enemy, if the throw misses).

In a Close-range encounter where one character shoots and the other uses melee, melee resolves first. Cyberware can make dodging melee easier, but blocking a melee attack requires an equal-or-greater melee weapon.

Explosives

Grenades, rockets, C4, and other demolitions are Primo Kit with the Boost #explosive, classified as High Explosive, Concussive, Fragmentation, Incendiary, Smoke, Gas, EMP, and so on. The type and size of effect is negotiated between GM and Player both when purchased/built and when used; real-world application of a prepped explosive is almost always more dynamic than what the Player anticipated, and that negotiation is meant to make space for the nuance. Explosives take up equipment slots appropriate to their size; buying or building them usually nets 3 of the specified type.

Bad guys & morale

A lowly gang member might only require 1✱ to defeat; others, particularly tough, cybered-up, or clever, might require 5✱ or more. Several low-level Baddies can be tracked together as a single group, reducing in effectiveness until their total ✱ is depleted.

As Baddies take ✱, consider whether they're the type to value their lives enough to flee or surrender (most), or the type always ready to fight to the death (few). Assign a Morale score of 2 to 12 (2 cowardly or wise, 7 unafraid, 12 fearless) and roll 2d6: if the result is under the Morale Score, the Baddie stays in the fight; equal or higher, they try to flee or surrender. Common Morale checks: the first time a Baddie takes ✱, and once they've taken more than half their total. Even staying in a fight, they're likely no longer at full capacity (#wounded, #glitchedcyberware, #outofrockets).

Social 'combat'

Social encounters between PCs and NPCs use a Task Roll like any other challenge, with PCs needing a number of ✱ to persuade, convince, intimidate, or charm an NPC into doing (or not doing) something. If the GM is unsure of an NPC's attitude, roll on the table below for a suggested attitude and Challenge dice.

PART 06 // JACK IN

The Net

There is no remote hacking. There is only the wire, the room, and what's plugged into it.

Accessing digital data and tools a character wouldn't normally have access to requires Hacking. Hacking a network remotely has become nearly impossible, so physical access to a device on the target network is required to reach its functions and other devices on it.

Old skool

Anyone can attempt to hack into most digital devices to access their intended functions (personal email, schedules, client contact info, if hacking a receptionist's terminal). Hacking this way can sometimes reach other devices on the same network, but only in ways those devices are already intended to connect (a security terminal accessing cameras and door locks, that same receptionist's terminal reaching a related database server).

VR breaching

PCs with the Neural Interface Cyberware installed can plug a cable directly from their body into almost any digital device (a computer terminal, an access panel, directly into an enemy's chip port) and instantly overlay their consciousness with a VR representation of the device's features and surrounding network. From there, pros can bypass the normal UI, directly manipulating the code underneath: gaining access not only to the device's functions, but potentially any other device on the same local network.

1✱ is always required to Breach a Network through a device. Once breached, the PC declares what they wish to achieve, and the GM either declares how many Hits that will require, describes any decisions the PC must make first, or explains the result isn't achievable right now or from this location.

Typical local networks: a single-family home, a retail store, a small commercial building, one floor of a large commercial building, one highly secured room, a security team, a gang of street thugs and their ride. It's up to the GM how large a network is and what's immediately accessible. (A gang might all be networked, but only their driver connected to the vehicle — decrypting that connection would be its own step before remote control of the car is possible.) Never forget that the party is likely networked too, and potentially just as vulnerable.

During Hacking attempts, add dice to the Challenge Pool according to the network's Security Level:

VR consequences

PCs who meet Consequences while hacking are likely running into ICE (Intrusion Countermeasure Electronics) protecting the network. These digital defenses can trace, disconnect, or even injure or kill a trespassing user. When a hacker meets a Consequence, take the number of Successes rolled in the Challenge Pool, add the Player's current Noise, and reference the table below.

  • Noise is the signal any user inevitably generates navigating a system. Each Task Roll generates 1 Noise. When a roll generates a Consequence, add current Noise to the Successes rolled in the Challenge pool and reference the VR Consequences table. Jacking out resets Noise to zero (though Consequences already suffered remain).
  • RED ICE are AI protocols that actively pursue and attack the PC, doing so again every time the PC generates a Push or Miss on the network, until they jack out or crash the program by "spiking" it with corrupting code. Give it a cool name and declare how many ✱ are required to crash it.
  • BLACK ICE are AI protocols, similar to RED ICE, capable of physically damaging the hacker's interface, body, or brain. Technically illegal tools, used only by criminals or megacorps (though what's the difference, really?).
  • Software damage is an Injury that only affects the PC while hacking in VR. It's usually repaired by rebooting the Neural Interface's operating software, requiring the PC to jack out of any device and succeed at a Task Roll to locate and clear the corrupted code.

Programs

Programs are Primo Kit useful only while VR breaching. They add Boosts to Task Rolls, though each may be used only once before jacking out and clearing the Neural RAM (NRAM). Neural Interfaces usually start with 2 NRAM slots, allowing up to 2 Programs to be launched during a breach and used repeatedly for its duration. Additional NRAM Cyberware (2 slots at a time) increases this.

Copies of the same Program may be launched as separate instances, each needing their own NRAM slot but multiplying the Program's power. Beware: if ICE corrupts a Program, it kills every instance of it. Several different Programs that accomplish the same goal can mitigate this risk.

Designing programs

Like Cyberware, Programs should be conceived of in terms of the verbs they assist:

Attack ICE  ·  Hide from ICE/User  ·  Locate Device/File  ·  Breach Network  ·  Control Device  ·  Eject User  ·  Fry Device/User  ·  Decrypt/Download File  ·  Encrypt/Upload File  ·  Scan  ·  Analyze

Getting in

  • Crowbar — force the first lock on a hardened network open, adding a Boost to the initial Breach attempt (Breach Network, +1d6)
  • Bloodhound — sniff out a specific device or file already on the local network (Locate Device/File, +1d6)
  • Skeleton Key — crack a file's encryption and pull a copy before anyone notices it's gone (Decrypt/Download File, +1d6)
  • Deadbolt — lock a file behind fresh encryption, or push new data onto the network clean (Encrypt/Upload File, +1d6)
  • Puppet Strings — take direct control of a networked device, as if operating it by hand (Control Device, +1d6)

Fighting back and staying hidden

  • Widowmaker — launch corrupting code straight at a Red or Black ICE program (Attack ICE, +1d6)
  • Overload — push a dangerous current through the connection, frying whatever's on the other end (Fry Device/User, +1d6)
  • Bouncer — forcibly disconnect another user from the network, mid-session (Eject User, +1d6)
  • Ghost Step — mask your presence on the network, quieter than the connection should allow (Hide from ICE/User, +1d6)
  • Wide Eyes — sweep the whole local network at once, revealing every device on it (Scan, +1d6)
  • Autopsy — tear apart a file, device, or program to learn exactly what it does and how it's protected (Analyze, +1d6)
PART 07 // ALTERED STATES

Chemistry & Memory

What you take, and what you did before the job started.

Drugs

Each dose lasts about 30–90 minutes and offers 1 specific Boost and 1 specific quirk. Multiple doses during that window multiply the effect, but risk an OD. Every use risks addiction, where related Tasks always carry an extra Challenge Die unless the drug is used. Dialysis can cure the addiction, and Cyberware can be installed that reduces or removes the risk of OD and addiction.

Duration — the GM decides when 30 minutes of in-game time has likely passed, then checks whether the effect has worn off after every scene (a change of venue, or a significant pause in the action). Roll 1d6 for the first duration check, and add 1d6 for each additional check. When a 6 is rolled, the effect has worn off.

OD — if the user takes an additional dose before the previous one wears off, roll 1d6 for each additional dose. On a 6, the user ODs and loses consciousness, waking in the next scene wherever the GM decides, with the Injury #OD.

Addiction — once a drug's effect wears off, roll 1d6. On a 6, the user is addicted. Addiction counts as an Injury that's only stabilized while on the drug (the user still gains its benefit in this state). The only cure is a DyaliSys™ machine, or going cold turkey for 3 play sessions.

Flashbacks

As with Blades in the Dark, you can perform an action "in the past" representing prep work done for the current gig. This often involves a Task Roll that could result in Consequences created during that prep, most commonly increased Heat.

The GM sets a Challenge rating when a Flashback is invoked:

  • +1 Challenge Die — an ordinary action for which you had easy opportunity.
  • +2 Challenge Dice — a complex action or unlikely opportunity.
  • +3 (or more) Challenge Dice — an elaborate action involving special opportunities or contingencies.

Players explain what they did in the past and the desired effect on the present, adding any relevant Boost. If they generate a Hit, things play out as described. Otherwise, things haven't gone as planned, and the GM explains what actually happened and raises the Riskometer as they see fit.

Limits of flashbacks

A Flashback isn't time travel. It can't undo something that just happened in the present moment. If a detective confronts you at a dive bar about recent robberies, you can't flash back to assassinate the detective the night before — he's here now, questioning you, that's established in the fiction. You could flash back to show you tipped him off intentionally, so he'd confront you at the bar, using that opportunity to impress the ganger boss watching from the railing above.

PART 08 // THE GRIND

Gigs, Downtime & the Long Con

The job, the payout, the patch-up, and the debt that never quite clears.

Gigs

A Gig is the basic unit of play in Hot Wired: a job, score, or mission the crew takes on, usually offered by a Fixer, a corporate handler working an angle, a gang contact calling in a favor, or some desperate Have-Not who scraped together enough Crypto to ask for help. Most sessions run one Gig from offer to payout.

  1. The Offer — the crew hears about the job and decides whether to take it.
  2. The Job — the crew executes, generating Hits, Consequences, and Heat along the way.
  3. The Payout — on success (or partial success), the crew gets paid.
  4. Downtime — the crew spends what they earned and prepares for what's next.
Payout

A Gig pays a base of ¢300 per PC on a clean success, modified by:

  • +¢100 per PC, for each tick of Heat the crew knowingly risked going in (dangerous jobs pay more)
  • +¢100 to ¢500 per PC, for cleanly completed bonus objectives (GM's call, scaled to difficulty)
  • −25% to the base payout if the central objective failed but the crew survived

A Suicide Mission pays, by definition, enough to clear the crew's debts and cover everyone's medical care.

Downtime

Between Gigs, the crew gets time to spend what they earned, patch themselves up, and get ready for whatever's next.

Each PC gets 2 Downtime Actions (GMs should adjust this for pacing). Each action is spent on one of the following:

  • Acquire or Build Kit — buy or build Primo Kit, Cyberware, Programs, Drugs, Mags, or Grenades.
  • Get Medical Treatment — heal Injuries or install Cyberware.
  • Reduce Heat — spend Crypto to buy down the crew's accumulated Heat.
  • Train — swap a Background or Skill Description for one that better fits who the character has become.
  • Lay Low — clear 1 Condition. No roll required, just describe how your character works through it in the gap between jobs.

PCs don't have to spend both actions the same way, and not every PC has to spend both. When the crew is done with Downtime, the next Gig begins.

Riskometer & Heat

Heat is the unit of danger and attention a crew accumulates during a Gig: noise made, witnesses left, alarms tripped, faces seen. The Riskometer is the 0–15 gauge that tracks it.

Add 1 Challenge Die to the Challenge Pool for every 5 Heat accumulated, rounded down. At 15 Heat, the current Gig collapses — narrate what happened (the crew gets made, the building locks down, the law shows up in force) and cut to the consequences.

Gaining Heat — the GM adds Heat once per Gig for each of the following, as fits the situation:

  • +1 Heat — Public use of firearms.
  • +1 Heat — Massive property damage.
  • +1 Heat — Destroying or stealing notable corporate assets.
  • +1 Heat (+1 more if the body was rich or notable) — Dropped bodies.
  • +1 or more Heat — Failed Flashbacks.
  • GM's call — Failures during a Gig often give the choice of Heat or a different Consequence instead.

Heat between Gigs — Heat does not reset on its own. It carries into the next Gig until bought down during Downtime (Reduce Heat, ¢50 per PC per point).

The suicide mission

In the rare event the whole crew is injured and broke, the heat is on, and there's no way out, there's always the one gig no one else in town was willing to take. The payout is enough to cover debts and get everyone medical attention. These missions don't resolve like normal Gigs: they play out through a series of narrated Task Rolls. Collectively, the group must roll a number of Hits equal to twice the number of players. Along the way, the first failure costs a piece of Primo Kit. The second results in an Injury. The third results in that character's death. The decision to accept a Suicide Mission must be unanimous.

Advancement

During Downtime, players may invest money from Gigs into Cyberware (or other Primo Kit) that grants new abilities and bonus dice, and may change Backgrounds and/or Skill Descriptions to ones they feel better fit the character they originally envisioned, or that better reflect what the character has become.
